The behavioral interview uses competency-based questions to understand how a candidate has handled real situations in the past. The AI interviewer asks questions rooted in the STAR format — Situation, Task, Action, Result — and probes for concrete details when answers are abstract or incomplete.

This interview style is designed to surface evidence of past behavior as a predictor of future performance. There are no right or wrong answers in an absolute sense; the AI is looking for specificity, structure, and self-awareness.

## STAR format questions

Every behavioral question invites the candidate to describe a specific past experience. The AI is designed to elicit responses that cover all four STAR components:

- **Situation** — What was the context? What was happening, and why did it matter?
- **Task** — What was the candidate specifically responsible for in that situation?
- **Action** — What did the candidate do, step by step? What decisions did they make?
- **Result** — What happened? What was the measurable or observable outcome?

The AI will prompt for missing components if the candidate omits them. For example, if the candidate describes a situation and their actions but does not mention an outcome, the AI will ask: _"What was the result of those actions?"_

<Callout title="Tip">
Candidates get the best scores by telling a specific story from their own experience, not a hypothetical or general description of how they "would" handle something. Use "I" rather than "we" when describing your role in the situation.
</Callout>

## Competencies evaluated

The behavioral interview assesses competencies defined in the scoring rubric. The default set includes:

| Competency | What the AI Looks For |
| --- | --- |
| Communication | Clear, structured, and audience-appropriate expression |
| Problem-Solving | Logical analysis, creative approaches, sound decision-making |
| Adaptability | Handling change, uncertainty, or unexpected obstacles |
| Teamwork | Collaboration, conflict resolution, supporting others |
| Leadership | Influencing outcomes, developing others, taking initiative |

Not every question targets every competency. Each question is tagged to one or two primary dimensions, and the AI scores only those dimensions for that question.

## How the AI prompts for detail

The AI's follow-up style in behavioral interviews is designed to feel like a supportive conversation, not an interrogation. Common prompts include:

- _"Can you give me a specific example of that?"_
- _"What was your personal role in that decision?"_
- _"How did that situation resolve?"_
- _"What would you do differently if you faced that situation again?"_

The AI does not challenge or push back on the substance of the candidate's answers — it asks neutral follow-up questions to draw out completeness and specificity.

## Tone and style

The behavioral AI interviewer uses a calm, professional, and encouraging tone. It acknowledges transitions between questions with brief phrases like _"Thank you, let's move to the next question"_ and maintains a consistent pace throughout. The style is intentionally conversational to reduce candidate anxiety and encourage authentic responses.

Candidates who feel anxious about talking to an AI should know that the AI is designed to be non-judgmental, does not show impatience, and will repeat or rephrase questions if asked.

## Session length

A standard behavioral screen contains four to six primary questions, each with up to two AI follow-ups depending on the configured follow-up depth. Typical session length is 20 to 30 minutes.

A Full Interview that includes both a behavioral and a technical section will run 40 to 60 minutes total. The AI announces the transition between sections: _"We've finished the behavioral section. Now I'd like to move to some technical questions."_

<Callout title="Note">
Session length varies based on how much the candidate speaks per answer. Candidates who give thorough, structured answers typically finish faster than those who give brief answers that trigger many follow-ups.
</Callout>
